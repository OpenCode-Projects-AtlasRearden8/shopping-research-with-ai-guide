# Chapter 1 · From vague want to sharp list

"I want a good laptop" is not a plan. It's a mood. The same goes for "a reliable fridge," "a decent mattress," or "a phone that just works." Every purchase you regret started as a vague sentence like that, followed by a shopping page that filled the blank for you.

This chapter fixes the blank. By the end you'll have a short list of criteria that any laptop, phone, appliance, or mattress has to pass, and you'll know exactly what "good" means for you. That list is the spine of everything else in this manual: it steers the AI prompts, the review reading, and the final decision.

## The five-question interview

Before any product names, get specific about life with the thing. Answer these in plain sentences, out loud if it helps:

1. **When and where will I actually use it?** ("On the couch at night," "in a tiny kitchen," "in a shared bedroom with a sensitive sleeper.")
2. **How often?** Daily use changes what matters. A laptop used three hours a day justifies fussing over keyboard and weight; a guest-room TV, less so.
3. **Who else touches it?** Partners, kids, pets, roommates. Shared objects need shared criteria.
4. **What does success look like in a month?** Not at the store. At home. "I stopped thinking about it" is a perfectly good answer; the best gear disappears into your life.
5. **What am I replacing, and why?** The complaint about the old one is usually your first MUST.

Question five is sneaky-useful. "The old mattress sagged in the middle" is worth more than fifty five-star reviews, because it tells you exactly which failure mode to guard against.

## The constraints (the boring half that saves you)

Now the unglamorous facts. Write these down even though you're sure you'll remember them; you won't:

- **The real ceiling.** Not "around a thousand"? Decide the number where the purchase stops being comfortable. That ceiling is also a filter: it disqualifies options early, which is the point.
- **Size and space.** Measure the doorway, the desk, the mattress frame, the kitchen alcove. Include the part that trips everyone: doors that have to swing open, and angled paths around corners.
- **The deadline.** "By March 15 when the old one dies" changes strategy completely. Deadlines trade money for time; know which side you're trading.
- **The return policy you'd actually use.** Write it down verbatim: how many days, who pays return shipping, restocking fees. Especially for mattresses; trials have fine print, covered in [Chapter 4](spec-sheets.html).

## The MUST / SHOULD / NICE ladder

Take your interview answers and constraints, and convert them into a table with three levels:

- **MUST**: if it fails this, I don't buy it, no matter how shiny the rest is. Cap this list at about four. A must-list of twelve is a wish-list wearing a disguise.
- **SHOULD**: worth real money to get. Break a SHOULD only if the price is great or a MUST demands it.
- **NICE**: genuinely pleasant, rarely decisive. Fine to lose; free to gain.

Then the step most people skip. Make each MUST **checkable**, meaning a stranger could verify it from a spec sheet or a review:

| Vague | Checkable |
| --- | --- |
| Fast laptop | 2025-or-newer chip, 16 GB RAM |
| Quiet dishwasher | Under 45 dB, per the spec sheet |
| Comfy mattress for side-sleeper | Medium firmness, foam-and-coil, 100+ night trial with free returns |
| Lasts a long time | 5+ years of OS updates promised by the maker |

"Checkable" is where research gets easy, because spec sheets and review summaries can answer it mechanically. Vague hopes can't be answered by anything, which is exactly why shopping pages are full of them.

## A worked example

"My laptop is slow and I take it everywhere."

- Interview: used daily on a commute and at a desk; types a lot; needs to survive a packed bag; battery dies by 2 pm now.
- Constraints: ceiling $900; must fit the current sleeve; buy within a month.
- MUSTs: under 1.4 kg; real-world all-day battery (say 8+ hours of typing and browsing); 16 GB RAM; fits the sleeve I already own.
- SHOULDs: 512 GB SSD; comfortable keyboard; screen bright enough for a sunny window.
- NICEs: backlit keys; a nice finish; Thunderbolt ports.

Notice what's absent: brand names. Criteria come before candidates, which is what keeps you from falling in love with whatever the homepage shows first.
